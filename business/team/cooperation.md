# Team Cooperation Guide
> AI-Powered Construction Procurement | Last updated: 2026-05-08
>
> Who works best with whom, how, and what to watch for.
> Covers core founders (5) and the two key insiders (Gerard, Dusko).

---

## How to use this document

This is not a personality assessment. It's a working manual for a team that is moving fast, partly distributed, and carrying two couple relationships inside a six-person founding structure. Read it before your first full team session, revisit it when tension emerges, and update it as you learn.

## Individual Profiles

---

### Christiaan - CEO / ENFP-T

**In collaboration he:**
- Generates energy and momentum. The team's natural accelerator - ideas come fast, connections happen fast, and he pulls people into possibility.
- Is a strong synthesiser: he can hold the vision and translate it into language that works for grant bodies, construction industry people, and tech investors simultaneously.
- Performs best with a clear agenda, but will ignore it productively if the conversation opens something better.
- Processes out loud. His thinking is not finished when he speaks it - treat early ideas as exploration, not commitment.

**What he needs from the team:**
- Someone to catch and track what gets decided in high-energy conversations (that's Kirsten's natural role). If she's not in the meeting, someone else needs to be the note-taker.
- Honest pushback from Milan when the idea outruns the execution reality. He respects directness more than diplomacy.
- Regular grounding check-ins - the ENFP-T turbulent edge means he can become self-critical when progress feels slow. The team should reflect wins explicitly back to him.

**Watch for:**
- Overcommitting externally before internal alignment. He will promise pilots, workshops, and partnerships at a rate that Kirsten and Milan need to sanity-check.
- Energy drops in execution phases. He is a builder and launcher - sustained administrative work drains him quickly. Kirsten handles this; don't put it on him.
- The "shiny new idea" pattern during periods of friction or slow progress. When the main thing feels stuck, he generates alternatives. Name this when it happens.

---

### Kirsten - COO / ENFJ-A

**In collaboration she:**
- Provides the operational backbone that Christiaan's energy requires. She catches the decisions, builds the systems, and holds the team to its own agreements.
- Is assertive and steady under pressure - ENFJ-A means she doesn't visibly wobble when things are hard, which makes her the de facto emotional regulator for the team.
- Leads through relationships and structure simultaneously. She is good at making people feel included in decisions while also moving things forward.
- Has real operational credibility from Open Food Chain (0 to 20 people, doubled revenue six consecutive years). She has done the hard part of this kind of company before.

**What she needs from the team:**
- Transparency. She can manage almost any situation with good information; she can't manage surprises. Tell her early when something is going sideways.
- Respect for the operational layer. The founders who don't have her background may underestimate how much work goes into keeping a company functional. Acknowledge it explicitly.
- Her own space in conversations - Christiaan's ENFP energy can inadvertently dominate. Meetings need structure that protects her contributions.

**Watch for:**
- Absorbing too much. ENFJ-A types are natural fixers; she will pick up whatever falls through the gaps. This is valuable but unsustainable at scale. Define what falls outside her remit.
- Friction with Milan. She is structured and process-oriented; he is strategic and systems-thinking but resistant to process he hasn't approved. This is the most likely recurring tension in the team. Keep it at the structural level, not the personal.
- The couple dynamic with Christiaan in team settings. They will sometimes need to visibly disagree in front of the group to signal that the business relationship is separate. This is healthy and should be normalised.

### Milan - CTO / INTJ-A

**In collaboration he:**
- Is the team's quality gate. His INTJ-A profile means he has very high internal standards, thinks in systems, and will not pretend something is better than it is. This is invaluable for an AI product that could easily oversell.
- Processes internally before speaking. When he is quiet, he is thinking - do not fill the silence. When he speaks, the thought is usually finished.
- Works best with clear ownership and clear deliverables. Ambiguous asks or recurring requests to "just check on things" will frustrate him.
- Is assertive - he will tell you if he disagrees. This is a feature. The team should treat his disagreement as a gift, not a conflict.

**What he needs from the team:**
- Respect for his technical judgement. He has production ML experience that the rest of the team does not. On questions of what is technically feasible, do not overrule him by committee.
- Decisions to actually be made and held. An INTJ's nightmare is a team that revisits settled questions. When Milan has moved on from a decision, reopening it without new information is corrosive.
- Space from Christiaan's idea generation rate. Milan needs to validate before committing. Create a lightweight "idea intake" process so new ideas don't become the default agenda of every meeting.

**Watch for:**
- Impatience with process and people management questions. He will disengage from meetings that don't have a clear technical or strategic purpose. Keep all-hands meetings short, focused, and consequential.
- The couple dynamic with Nina. He may unconsciously represent her technical views as his own, or filter his communication to the team through her. This reduces her direct voice - watch for it.
- Over-engineering in the early phase. INTJ types build for the correct solution; startups sometimes need the good-enough solution first. Kirsten and Christiaan need to explicitly name the "MVP constraint" to him.

### Nina - Domain Expert / Research

**In collaboration she:**
- Is the team's ground truth for construction engineering. Her 12 years in civil engineering academia and her PhD in structural engineering mean she understands the technical language, the workflow, and the cultural norms of construction procurement at a level no one else on the team has.
- Also connects the University of Belgrade to the project - as an Assistant Professor, she is the institutional anchor on the Serbian side (alongside the Windesheim anchor in NL). This is critical for the RAAK-PRO and Horizon Europe grant applications.
- Is an academic by training: she thinks in precise definitions, requires evidence before committing to claims, and will slow the team down when they overstate the product's capability. This is exactly what the team needs - and may sometimes feel like resistance.
- Works at a distance (Belgrade). Her best contribution comes through structured, well-prepared interactions rather than high-bandwidth real-time collaboration.

**What she needs from the team:**
- Clear, specific questions with enough lead time for her to research them properly. Don't ask her for domain validation in a 30-minute Zoom call.
- Her research contributions to be formally credited - in grant applications, in product documentation, and in external presentations. She is an academic; attribution matters and shapes her institutional relationship with the project.
- Direct communication about what the team needs from her, especially Christiaan and Kirsten, who are less connected to the Belgrade network. She should not have to guess the priorities.

**Watch for:**
- Distance making her peripheral. The NL team (Christiaan, Kirsten, Gerard) operates at a faster cadence than the Belgrade pair. Without deliberate inclusion, Nina's input arrives after decisions have already been shaped.
- Her voice being filtered through Milan. See Milan's note above - she should have her own communication channel to the broader team, separate from what goes through him.
- Academic conservatism in commercial settings. She may be uncomfortable making claims the research hasn't fully validated. This is correct and should be respected - the team should not pressure her to endorse what the product can't yet do.

### Gerard - Head of Government Sales / Advisor

**In collaboration he:**
- Brings 19 years of direct trust relationships across all 342 Dutch municipalities - this is not a database, it's a personal network. The relationship IS the asset.
- Understands the language, procurement culture, and decision-making rhythm of Dutch public institutions at a depth that no other team member has. He knows which procurement officer to call, how they evaluate risk, and what makes them move.
- Is best used for: opening municipality doors, validating pain points, preparing the team for what to expect in the public sector sales cycle, and acting as a reference validator once pilots are running.
- Has a corporate background (HPE, large organisation). He is not a startup founder and should not be treated as one. He needs clear briefs, clear asks, and clear feedback loops.

**What he needs from the team:**
- Very specific, well-prepared briefs before every municipality engagement. He knows the relationship side; Christiaan and Kirsten need to give him the product narrative and the specific ask in writing.
- Quick, clean follow-up after introductions. If he opens a door and the team doesn't walk through it promptly, the relationship cost falls on him. This is the highest risk in the collaboration.
- Respect for the pace of his network. Municipality relationships move at a different speed than startup conversations. Pushing for urgency where he has said patience is needed will damage the relationships.

**Watch for:**
- His corporate instincts shaping advice that doesn't fit a startup context. He may suggest processes or approaches designed for HPE's scale. Christiaan needs to translate, not just adopt.
- Commission misalignment. His compensation structure (base + commission) means he has an incentive to open doors; the team has an incentive to close them. Align the success criteria explicitly and in writing.
- The team not using him actively enough. He is available and connected; the most common failure mode with advisors like Gerard is under-use, not over-use.

### Dusko - Züblin Insider / Advisor

**In collaboration he:**
- Is the closest thing to a product advisory user the team currently has. His current role - Project Lead for Bid Processing (turnkey construction) at ZÜBLIN Stuttgart - means he processes construction procurement bids as his primary job. The product is built for someone exactly like him.
- Also has a full career background in cost control, quantity surveying, and tendering across STRABAG and ZÜBLIN - which means he can validate both the problem (accurate) and the proposed solution (plausible/not plausible) from real experience.
- Shares the Serbian engineering network with Milan and Nina (MSc from Faculty of Civil Engineering, University of Belgrade). This is a trust shortcut - the relationship is warm, not transactional.
- Has Python and Java certifications alongside his engineering credentials - tech-curious enough to engage with the product at a technical level, not just a user level.

**What he needs from the team:**
- To be engaged as a validator and advisor, not as a sales target. His help with the product is more valuable than a rushed pilot commitment.
- Clear separation between his personal involvement (voluntary advisor, domain expert) and his company's potential involvement (ZÜBLIN as a grant industry partner or pilot client). He cannot commit ZÜBLIN without proper internal process - do not put him in that position.
- Regular, low-stakes contact to stay warm. He has a demanding job. The best way to keep him engaged is short, specific check-ins with clear asks, not long open-ended conversations.

**Watch for:**
- Confidentiality boundaries. Dusko works inside ZÜBLIN's bid processing workflow. Any specific procurement data, pricing logic, or internal process information he shares needs to be handled carefully - it belongs to his employer, not to the startup.
- The MIT R&D grant co-applicant question. ZÜBLIN/STRABAG is a major construction group - they are NOT an SME and cannot be the SME co-applicant required by the MIT R&D AI grant. For MIT R&D, a different SME partner is needed. ZÜBLIN can be a named industry partner in KIEM and RAAK-PRO.
- Milan managing all communication to Dusko. Given their Serbian network connection, Milan is the natural contact. But if this becomes the only channel, Christiaan loses the ability to build his own relationship with ZÜBLIN/Dusko - which matters for the long-term partnership.

## Key Pairings

---

### Christiaan + Milan (CEO + CTO) - The Core Tension

This is the most important relationship in the company and the most likely source of productive friction.

**The dynamic:** ENFP-T (visionary, ideas-first, possibility-oriented) meets INTJ-A (systems thinker, execution-first, high standards). Classically complementary - and classically combustible.

**What works:**
- Christiaan generates the commercial and strategic direction; Milan designs the system to get there. When both are clear on their lane, output is high.
- Milan's directness is exactly what Christiaan needs - he doesn't want to be managed gently. Blunt technical feedback lands better than diplomatic hedging.
- Christiaan's energy brings Milan's work into rooms where it needs to go (investors, grants, customers). Milan needs this even when he doesn't enjoy it.

**What breaks:**
- Christiaan presenting to external parties what Milan has not confirmed as technically sound. This erodes Milan's trust quickly.
- Milan making technical decisions that affect product direction without looping Christiaan. He will assume technical authority extends further than Christiaan expects.
- Either one re-raising decisions already made. Milan finds it corrosive; Christiaan will do it when he has new information (or thinks he does).

**Working agreements to establish:**
1. No external capability claims without Milan's written sign-off on the technical statement
2. A weekly 30-minute sync (just the two of them) - before the week starts, not as a debrief
3. A "parking lot" for new ideas that don't touch this week's priorities

---

### Christiaan + Kirsten (CEO + COO, also partners)

**The dynamic:** ENFP-T + ENFJ-A. Both NF types, both people-oriented, both strong communicators. High natural alignment - which also means their disagreements are invisible to the rest of the team.

**What works:**
- Kirsten grounds Christiaan's output into deliverable form. She is his most reliable converter of vision to action.
- They process decisions faster than any other pair. They can align in five minutes on something that would take the full team an hour.
- Mutual trust means they can take risks together (first pilots, grant applications, partnership commitments) without the friction that slows down less-aligned co-founders.

**What breaks:**
- The team cannot tell when they're operating as co-founders vs as a couple. This creates a 2v3 dynamic (perceived NL bloc vs Serbia/Gerard) that fragments decisions. Be visibly transparent about when you disagree.
- If Kirsten pre-aligns with Christiaan before team meetings, the rest of the team feels like they're being managed to a conclusion rather than consulted. Leave genuine open questions actually open.
- Kirsten absorbs things she shouldn't to protect Christiaan. This is not sustainable and will cause her to burn out before the team notices.

**Working agreements to establish:**
1. No pre-alignment on major decisions before team meetings - or if you've pre-aligned, say so explicitly
2. One operational decision per week that Kirsten makes without Christiaan's input (builds her independent authority)
3. A personal agreement (between the two of you, not in a work document) about what's off-limits in business conversations

---

### Milan + Nina (CTO + Domain Expert, also partners)

**The dynamic:** INTJ-A (systems thinker, high standards, assertive) + academic domain expert. On paper: the CTO who builds the AI + the engineer who knows what it needs to know. In practice: two people who process problems deeply, prefer careful language, and share a personal life.

**What works:**
- Their shared University of Belgrade background and Serbian network (Nina, Dusko) gives them a direct line into the construction engineering world that the NL team doesn't have.
- Milan can translate Nina's domain expertise into technical requirements in ways no one else can - the shortest path from "construction procurement reality" to "model training data" runs through their relationship.
- They provide a natural quality gate on both sides - Milan on technical claims, Nina on domain claims.

**What breaks:**
- Nina's voice disappearing into Milan's. When the team hears from "Milan + Nina" as a unit, Nina's independent domain expertise is discounted. She needs her own channel.
- The distance problem. Nina is in Belgrade. Most fast-moving decisions happen in NL time, in NL timezone. Milan participates; Nina hears about it after. Create a deliberate structure to include her before decisions, not after.
- Couple conflict affecting technical collaboration. An INTJ under stress becomes colder and more withdrawn. If the personal relationship is under pressure, Milan's team communication will be the first casualty.

**Working agreements to establish:**
1. Nina has her own standing agenda item in team meetings - not "Milan and Nina update"
2. Any domain validation request goes to Nina directly (with cc to Milan), not through Milan
3. Belgrade team to explicitly block off EU timezone overlap hours for synchronous work

### The NL Bloc + the Belgrade Pair - Managing the Geographic Split

Five out of seven people are in the Netherlands (or Germany). Two are in Belgrade. This creates a natural drift toward NL-centric decisions unless deliberately countered.

**Practical rules:**
- Schedule all full-team meetings in Belgrade afternoon / NL mid-day. Do not schedule late NL afternoon calls (they push into Belgrade evening).
- Belgrade pair should receive meeting agendas 24 hours in advance. NL team often decides in ad hoc conversations; Belgrade team needs preparation time.
- Any decision with product or research implications should not be finalised until Nina has had the opportunity to respond - even asynchronously.
- Acknowledge the timezone asymmetry explicitly. It is not that Nina and Milan are less available; it is that the current structure disadvantages distributed team members.

## Advisor Relationships: Gerard and Dusko

Both Gerard and Dusko are insiders, not co-founders. The key risk with both is the same: they are valuable because of who they are and where they are, which means the team cannot afford to damage those relationships through poor management.

**Shared principles for both:**
- Every request to Gerard or Dusko should have a clear ask, a clear timeframe, and a clear feedback loop. Vague asks waste their time and signal disorganisation.
- Thank them explicitly and specifically after every significant contribution. They are not on the full founder compensation structure; recognition is part of what keeps them engaged.
- Do not treat them as employees. They have their own jobs, their own reputations to protect, and their own limits on what they can commit.

**Gerard: specific**
- Christiaan owns the Gerard relationship on behalf of the company. Kirsten handles the logistics; no one else contacts Gerard for asks without Christiaan's awareness.
- Every municipality introduction should be preceded by a one-pager (product + ask) reviewed by Gerard before any meeting.
- Pay him on success, not on activity. Define "success" together and in writing.

**Dusko: specific**
- Milan owns the Dusko relationship as the primary contact. Christiaan should build his own secondary relationship over time.
- Any use of Dusko's domain knowledge in public materials (pitch deck, grant applications) should be cleared with him first.
- Do not involve ZÜBLIN institutionally (as a grant partner, pilot client, or reference) without Dusko explicitly saying his manager is aware and supportive.

## Red Flags to Watch For - Team-Level

These are patterns that suggest a structural problem, not just a bad day:

| Pattern | What it signals | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Milan goes quiet for more than 3 days during a sprint | INTJ withdrawal - either blocked technically or frustrated interpersonally | Direct one-to-one with Christiaan, not a team meeting |
| Christiaan mentions three new directions in one week | Avoidance of a hard problem in the current focus | Name it directly: "What's the thing we're not moving on?" |
| Kirsten stops flagging problems | She's overloaded and has stopped expecting the team to help | Reduce her scope, not increase her resilience |
| Nina's responses become very short | She feels peripheral or her input wasn't acted on | Review the last 3 weeks of decisions - was she included? |
| Gerard stops initiating contact | He's waiting for a follow-up the team forgot to send | Audit open loops from his last introduction |
| Dusko becomes cautious and vague | Something at ZÜBLIN has made his involvement more sensitive | Don't push. Give him an easy out and wait for him to re-engage |

## The Couple Dynamic: A Direct Word

Two of the five co-founders are romantic partners with each other, and both partnerships are within the same team. This is unusual and worth naming plainly.

**It is not a dealbreaker.** Couples have built successful companies. What matters is that the business has clear rules that don't depend on the personal relationships being smooth.

**The practical requirement:**
- Co-founder agreement must be signed - including vesting, equity, exit, and decision rights - before the first commercial contract. This protects everyone regardless of the personal relationship.
- Both couples should agree in private how they will signal disagreement in team settings. If you always appear aligned, the rest of the team cannot tell whether a decision was actually contested.
- If a personal conflict is affecting work capacity, the affected founder should say so plainly ("I need to take this week lighter") rather than masking it. The team is small enough that everyone will notice anyway.

**What investors and grant reviewers will ask:**
"What happens if one of the couples separates?" The answer should be: "The co-founder agreement covers equity and decision rights independently of the personal relationship. The business is not structurally dependent on either couple remaining together."

Have that answer ready and documented. Not because it's likely, but because a team that has thought about it signals maturity.
